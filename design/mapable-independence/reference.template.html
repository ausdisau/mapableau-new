01 / Our approach

Confidence, not complexity.

Disabled participants are decision-makers. The interface should reflect that, at every step.

“Support my next step.
Don’t decide it for me.”

A design principle, not a participant testimonial.

  1. Make the next step clear. Use plain language and one clear primary action.
  2. Make permission specific. Say who receives what, why, and for how long.
  3. Keep a person in reach. Automation never implies authority or replaces safeguarding.
  4. Design for different ways of using. Keyboard, touch, zoom, assistive technology and time to think.
The logo is an artwork, not a kit of parts

Keep the supplied square image whole, including its white background, gradient pin, landscape, wordmark and tagline. Never crop it into an icon, remove the background or rebuild the tagline. Give it space. If a compact header cannot fit the complete artwork legibly, change the layout—not the logo.

06 / Choice & authority

Permission is specific.

A relationship is not blanket authority. Consent, authority verification and coordination are different things.

Illustrative only / Local consent pattern

Example sharing is off. No choice has been recorded.

Consent is not a pre-ticked box.

Keep choices separate. Explain refusal, withdrawal and any service impact honestly. Never infer agreement from silence.

Authority not verified

A coordinator can help.
They cannot assume.

Show who is acting, on whose behalf, and the scope and expiry of verified authority. A support relationship alone does not grant permission.

Production needs server-side checks and an auditable record. A badge, checkbox or chatbot response cannot confer authority.

Accessible places: show the source, review date and any uncertainty for access information. “Step-free” does not mean every access need is met.

07 / Access by default

Different needs.
Equal consideration.

Accessibility is a continuous practice, not a theme switch or a compliance badge.

Interaction

  • 48px minimum control targets; let labels wrap.
  • Visible, offset focus that does not depend on gold on cream.
  • Every field has a label and associated help or error text.
  • Never communicate risk or permission through colour alone.
  • Use native keyboard behaviour; no custom shortcut traps.

Perception & comfort

  • Normal text pairs checked at 4.5:1 or better.
  • Readable font and line-height preferences.
  • Reduced-motion support; no animated loading pulse.
  • Forced-colour outlines and borders remain visible.
  • Responsive reflow; check 200% text and 400% zoom in the consuming page.
Try the keyboard.

Tab through the appearance controls, open a disclosure with Enter or Space, and explore the example choices. Native focus order follows the reading order.

08 / Build with it

One considered foundation.

Framework-neutral React. Scoped CSS. A deliberate migration—not a silent replacement.

Opt in, don’t overwrite.

Import the stylesheet once from the Next.js root layout. Wrap the intended surface in .mapable-system. Choose a theme on that same element.

<section className="mapable-system" data-ma-theme="light"> <Button>Review choices</Button> </section>

Integrate with care.

The canonical app currently has teal/green tokens and separate compact logo treatments. Those are not modified here. An optional shadcn bridge maps inherited semantic tokens for an opted-in subtree.

The original square artwork needs more header space than the old compact lockup. Deploy the byte-identical asset; don’t squeeze, crop or rebuild it.

Source, build and limitations

Source lives in design/mapable-independence/. Read docs/AGENTS.md before making another artifact. Run node design/mapable-independence/build.mjs --check to check tokens, source/asset parity and contrast pairs. React primitives require React 18+ and existing React types. Backend consent enforcement, authority verification, audit persistence, help routing and real status announcements belong to the host application.
